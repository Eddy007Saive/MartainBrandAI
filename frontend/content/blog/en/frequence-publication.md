The question comes up every time, and it is the wrong one. "How many times a week should I post?" assumes there is a magic number. There isn't. There is a floor below which an account goes quiet, a ceiling above which your audience tunes out, and — above all — a pace **you** can hold for twelve months straight. That last one decides everything.

## The short answer, network by network

| Network | Comfortable pace | Bare minimum | What matters most |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LinkedIn | 3 to 5 posts / week | 1 per week | Consistency and comments |
| Instagram (feed) | 3 to 4 / week | 2 per week | Visual quality |
| Instagram (stories) | every working day | 3 per week | Presence, not production |
| Facebook (page) | 2 to 3 / week | 1 per week | Native formats, never a bare link |
| TikTok / Reels | 4 to 7 / week | 3 per week | Volume, genuinely |

These ranges are starting points, not laws. A law firm publishing one thorough analysis a week on LinkedIn will beat a competitor posting five platitudes. A tradesperson showing three job sites a week in stories will beat a perfectly polished account updated once a month.

## Why consistency beats volume

Three reasons, in order of importance.

**Algorithms reward predictability.** An account that publishes on a steady rhythm gets more stable distribution than one alternating between bursts and silence. There is no magic to it: the platform learns who to show your content to, and that learning decays when you disappear for three weeks.

**Your audience forms a habit.** The people following you don't consciously note your rhythm, but they absorb it. Posting on Tuesdays and Thursdays for two months creates an appointment. Posting seven times in three days and then nothing creates noise.

**Consistency compounds.** Thirty average posts spread over three months produce more than thirty average posts published in three weeks, because each one reaches a different slice of your audience and the effects stack.

## How to find your own pace

### 1. Measure your real capacity, not your imagined one

Time yourself once: how long does one post take, from idea to publication? Visual included, proofreading included. Most independents land somewhere between 40 and 90 minutes. Multiply by the frequency you have in mind. If the total exceeds what you can genuinely free up each week, the frequency is wrong — however "recommended" it may be.

### 2. Halve that number

It is counter-intuitive and it is the most useful advice on this page. The pace you will hold during a busy week, on holiday, in the middle of an urgent project, is roughly half the one you imagine while planning on a motivated Sunday evening.

### 3. Hold it for eight weeks before judging

Two months is the minimum needed to tell a signal from an accident. Below that, you are only measuring variance. After eight weeks there are two outcomes: either the pace is comfortable and you can step it up, or it costs too much and you should reduce it — not abandon it.

## The three most expensive mistakes

**The burst followed by silence.** Five posts in one week, then a month of nothing. It is the most common pattern and the costliest: you pay the price of production without collecting the reward of consistency.

**The same content copied identically everywhere.** What works on LinkedIn — long text, an argument — dies on Instagram. That isn't a pacing problem, but it becomes one: duplicating feels like publishing a lot while genuinely reaching no one.

**Posting for the algorithm.** Publishing because "it's been three days" produces content with nothing to say, and your audience feels it before you do. Better to skip a day than to publish emptiness.

## What if you can't hold the pace?

Three levers, from cheapest to most expensive.

**Batch the production.** Writing six posts in one two-hour session takes far less time than six twenty-minute sessions: you pay the cost of getting back into context only once.

**Recycle without shame.** Content that worked well six months ago was seen by a fraction of your audience. Reworded, it will work again. Accounts that publish a lot do not create everything from scratch.

**Get help from a tool.** That is Postorico's role: producing drafts in your voice, scheduling them, publishing at the right time. You keep the decision and the final read — which is what stops the result from sounding generic — but you no longer start from a blank page every time.

## What to take away

The right pace is the one that survives a hard week. Start below the recommended frequency, hold it for two months, then increase. An account posting twice a week for a year consistently beats an account that posted daily for six weeks before going dark.
